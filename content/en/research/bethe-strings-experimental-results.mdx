Bethe strings are one of the most counterintuitive predictions in quantum many-body physics: clusters of particles that stay bound in a one-dimensional gas where ordinary intuition says attraction should trigger collapse. The newest **Bethe strings experimental results**, published in *Nature Communications* by an Innsbruck-led collaboration working with ultracold cesium, turn that prediction into a controlled laboratory phenomenon. These **Bethe strings experimental results** provide strong indirect evidence: the team created dense mixtures of bound states of different sizes, then found consistent signatures across three independent observables.

Why does it matter? Because Bethe strings sit at the crossroads of integrable quantum systems, anomalous transport, quantum magnetism, and even string theory. Being able to realize and probe dense out-of-equilibrium intermixtures is a prerequisite for testing any of those ideas in the lab.

## What Are Bethe Strings, Exactly?

The concept traces back to Hans Bethe's 1931 solution of a one-dimensional spin chain, and to the exactly solvable Lieb-Liniger model of bosons interacting through a contact potential. In that model, eigenstates are labeled not by ordinary momenta but by *rapidities* — generalized momenta of stable quasiparticles.

In the repulsive regime, rapidities are real numbers. Push the interaction to strong attraction and something unusual happens: rapidities acquire imaginary parts and organize into vertical clusters sharing the same real part, evenly spaced along the imaginary axis. Each cluster is a Bethe string — a genuine multiparticle bound state with a well-defined size *n*.

| Regime | Interaction | Rapidity structure | Behavior of the gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quasi-condensate | weak repulsive | real, densely packed | weakly interacting gas |
| Tonks-Girardeau | strong repulsive | real, spread out | bosons fermionize |
| Super Tonks-Girardeau | quench to strong attraction | real, metastable | excited gas without bound states |
| String regime | attractive, finite rate | complex, clustered | Bethe strings of various sizes |

Two numbers define a string of size *n*: its spatial extent and its binding energy. The bond length shrinks roughly as 1/[*c*|(*n*−1)], while the binding energy grows steeply with size, scaling like *n*(*n*²−1). Bigger strings are tighter and harder to break — a fact that shapes every measurement below.

## How the Experiment Realizes a String Intermixture

The platform is a Bose-Einstein condensate of cesium atoms loaded into two crossed optical lattice beams. The beams carve the cloud into thousands of independent one-dimensional tubes, each holding a few to a few dozen atoms. A broad Feshbach resonance lets the team dial the interaction strength with a magnetic field.

The key move is a finite-rate sweep of the interaction from repulsive, through the non-interacting point, into the attractive regime. An adiabatic sweep would collapse the gas into a single giant cluster; a fast one would leave a far-from-equilibrium mess. The intermediate rate used here produces a distribution of strings of many sizes.

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Atoms per tube | 7–30 |
| Populated tubes | 3,000–5,000 |
| 1D temperature | 3–35 nK |
| Lattice depth | 25 *E*ᵣ (20 *E*ᵣ for one dataset) |
| Transverse trap frequency | 10.5 kHz |
| Longitudinal trap frequency | 29.3 Hz |
| Starting interaction | 3.4 μm⁻¹ |
| Ramp rate | ≈ −0.038 μm⁻¹ ms⁻¹ |

For a string of size *n* = 4 at an interaction of −1 μm⁻¹, the bond length is about 0.67 μm and the binding energy is roughly *k*_B × 9 nK. That is small but measurable — provided you know where to look.

## Bethe Strings Experimental Results: The Release Energy Evidence

The detection protocol is a two-step time-of-flight sequence. First the cloud expands inside the tubes for a set time; then the transverse confinement is switched off and the gas expands freely in 3D. Comparing the two stages isolates what happens to bound states.

The reasoning is clean. During 1D expansion, strings scatter but stay intact, so the measured release energy reflects only their center-of-mass motion. When the tubes are opened, the confinement vanishes, the strings break apart, and their binding energy converts into kinetic energy. The 3D release energy should therefore be larger — but only if bound states exist.

| Interaction regime | 1D release energy | 3D release energy | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strongly repulsive | ≈ *h* × 100 Hz | ≈ *h* × 100 Hz | No bound states; both equal internal energy |
| Near non-interacting | ≈ *h* × 60 Hz | ≈ *h* × 60 Hz | Agreement persists |
| *c* = −1.0 μm⁻¹ | plateaus near *h* × 35 Hz | rises to ≈ *h* × 110 Hz | Binding energy released only in 3D |

That gap is the headline of the Bethe strings experimental results. The difference between the two energies gives an average binding energy per particle, and from it the team extracted a conservative lower bound: the gas must contain strings of at least six particles. Refined modeling predicts even larger ones.

## Momentum Distribution and the Role of Temperature

A second line of evidence comes from the momentum distribution after 1D expansion. Starting from a broad, bell-shaped profile in the repulsive regime, the distribution narrows as the interaction approaches zero, then broadens sharply once the gas crosses into attraction.

| Interaction *c* (μm⁻¹) | Momentum width (FWHM) | Trend |
|---|---|---|
| Repulsive start | 6.0(1) ℏ μm⁻¹ | broad |
| 0.1 | 3.0(1) ℏ μm⁻¹ | narrowest point |
| −2.0 | nearly double the minimum | clear broadening |

The broadening has an intuitive reading. A string with bond length Δ*z* releases momentum over a window set by the uncertainty principle, roughly inversely proportional to that length. Larger strings and stronger attraction produce wider momentum spreads.

Temperature matters just as much. At higher 1D temperatures, kinetic energy dominates over interaction energy, so large bound states become statistically unlikely. The measured widths confirm this: the broadening effect weakens as temperature rises, exactly as generalized hydrodynamics predicts.

## Tan's Contact and Short-Range Correlations

The third probe targets short-range correlations directly. Bethe strings change the probability of finding two particles at the same point, a quantity captured by Tan's contact — a universal parameter linking microscopic detail to thermodynamics.

Using the virial theorem, the contact can be extracted from the internal energy and the trap's potential energy. The team found that this relation, normally stated for thermal equilibrium, also holds for the out-of-equilibrium states described by generalized hydrodynamics. The Bethe strings experimental results therefore extend a textbook relation into a genuinely dynamical setting.

## Losses, Limits, and What Comes Next

No experiment is perfectly integrable. The dominant symmetry-breaking process here is three-body loss, which grows rapidly as strings tighten at stronger attraction.

| Quantity | Value | How obtained |
|---|---|---|
| 1D loss coefficient *k*₃ | 1.10(3) × 10⁻⁸ cm² s⁻¹ | fit to atom-loss curves |
| Implied 3D coefficient | 1.50(4) × 10⁻²⁷ cm⁶ s⁻¹ | transverse wavefunction integration |
| Direct 3D measurement nearby | ≈ 3 × 10⁻²⁷ cm⁶ s⁻¹ | prior literature |

The agreement is close enough to confirm that three-body loss, not some exotic new mechanism, explains the small deviations between data and simulation at strong attraction. Larger strings disappear first because they are more tightly bound.

So what comes next? Three directions stand out. First, denser and more controllable mixtures of string sizes would let researchers test how strings interact with one another. Second, the same physics appears in quantum magnets and spin chains, where large strings are thought to drive anomalous superdiffusion and Kardar-Parisi-Zhang scaling. Third, the techniques demonstrated here — interaction ramps plus rapidity-resolved imaging — are reusable for other integrable systems.

The paper states that experimental and simulation data are available on Zenodo, which should accelerate follow-up work. For a field that waited nearly a century for evidence, that openness matters almost as much as the result itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What are Bethe strings in simple terms?**
They are bound clusters of particles in a one-dimensional quantum gas with attractive interactions. Instead of collapsing, the particles form stable groups of two, three, or many members, each described by a set of complex rapidities in the Bethe ansatz solution.

**Why are the latest Bethe strings experimental results considered a milestone?**
Earlier work inferred strings indirectly, mainly in magnetic materials. This experiment created a dense mixture of different string sizes in a cold-atom gas and found consistent evidence across three independent measurements: release energy, momentum distribution, and Tan's contact.

**How large can a Bethe string get?**
In principle, the ground state of *N* attractively interacting particles is a single string with *N* constituents. The experiment measured a lower bound of six particles per string and theory predicts a broad distribution extending larger, set mainly by temperature and density.

**Do these results have practical applications?**
Not directly in the short term. Their value is foundational: they validate generalized hydrodynamics in a strongly correlated quantum system and provide a controllable testbed for ideas about anomalous transport, quantum magnetism, and integrable dynamics.
